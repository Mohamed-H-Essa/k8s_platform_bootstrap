# Postmortem: chaos day, 2026-10-30

**Status:** complete · **Format:** blameless · **Environment:** local kind cluster (OrbStack,
8 CPU / 8 GB, macOS host) · **Load:** k6 at 50 requests/s through the gateway the whole time
(85 % redirects, 10 % creates, 5 % stats) · **How to repeat:** [chaos-day.md](../chaos-day.md)

All times are local (UTC+2) and come from the scripts' timeline (`.local/chaos/<run>/timeline.log`),
the per-request k6 log and Prometheus. Numbers are from one laptop that also ran the load
generator; they show behaviour, not capacity.

## Summary
We injected five events in two runs. A normal release, a pod crash, a database failover and a bad
release behaved as designed. **Losing a worker node did not**: in run 1, 48 % of requests failed
for 6 minutes, the application's own metrics saw none of it, and the application's pods on the lost
node were never replaced. Two platform components with a single replica and the default 5-minute
eviction delay were the cause. We fixed them the same day and ran the experiment again (run 2).

## Impact (run 1, node loss)
- 12:05:09 → 12:11:12 (6 min 3 s): **8,778 of 18,140 requests failed (48.4 %)**: Envoy answered
  503, or the request timed out after k6's 5 s.
- The availability SLO (99.5 %, measured at the application) recorded **zero** errors: the
  failures were generated by the gateway and never reached a shortener pod. No alert fired.
- Capacity: the HPA wanted 4 shortener pods; 2 ran until the node came back.
- In budget terms: at 50 req/s a 30-day window has ~129.6 M requests, so the 0.5 % budget is
  ~648 k failed requests; this incident spent 8,778 (1.4 %) at a burn rate of ~97×. Even if the
  SLI had seen it, the page (1 h ratio above 7.2 %) would not have fired: over the hour the error
  ratio peaks near 4.9 %. That is the design (a 6-minute blip that spends 1.4 % of the month is
  not worth waking someone), but it means short, sharp outages need their own signal.

## What went well
- Releases are boring: two releases under load, 0 failed requests, ~45 s from `make release` to
  published and 35–48 s to rolled out (graceful shutdown + `maxUnavailable: 0` work).
- A crashed pod costs nothing at this traffic: 0 failed requests, replaced in 12 s.
- Prometheus and Alertmanager were deliberately on the control-plane node, so all the evidence
  survived the node loss.
- The scripts measured everything with timestamps; nobody had to read a graph by eye.

## What went wrong, and why
1. **The gateway's data plane kept routing to dead pods for 6 minutes.** Envoy gets its endpoints
   from the Envoy Gateway controller. The single controller replica ran on the lost node and, like
   every pod without an explicit toleration, stayed bound to it for the default 300 s after
   NotReady. Envoy had no health checks or retries of its own, so about half of the traffic went to
   the two dead pods (503s), and connections to them waited out Envoy's default 10 s connect
   timeout (k6 timeouts).
2. **Kyverno's fail-closed webhook blocked pod eviction.** Its only admission controller was also
   on the lost node. The policies matched Pods without listing operations, so the webhook also
   received DELETE requests, and with Kyverno unreachable the API server refused the taint
   eviction controller's deletes. The eviction was not retried; the ReplicaSet never replaced the
   pods.
3. **The SLI could not see it.** The availability SLO is measured from the application's metrics.
   Gateway-generated 503s and client timeouts never reach the application.
4. While investigating we also found that the availability recording rule returned *no data*
   (instead of 0 %) whenever there had been no 5xx at all (`sum()` over no series), which would
   also hide a healthy service on the dashboard.

## Lessons
- Graceful paths (rollouts, crashes) were tested by design; the ungraceful one (a node vanishing)
  exposed the platform's own single points of failure, not the application's.
- A fail-closed admission webhook is part of the availability path for everything it matches,
  including deletes. Scope it to what it must see.
- An SLI measured inside the service is blind to failures in front of it. Measure where the
  user is, too.
